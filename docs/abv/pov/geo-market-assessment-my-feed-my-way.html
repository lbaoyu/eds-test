Executive assessment

A weak direct catalyst, a meaningful longer-term signal

Australia’s proposed Digital Duty of Care, including the “My Feed, My Way” initiative, should be viewed as a weak direct catalyst for the GEO market, but a potentially meaningful longer-term signal about the regulation and governance of algorithmic and AI-mediated experiences.

The proposal has two relevant components

First

Social-media platforms would have to give Australians over 16 a choice between a personalized, algorithmically recommended default feed and a feed based on friends and creators they choose to follow.

Second

The broader Digital Duty of Care extends beyond social media to services including AI chatbots, apps and games, particularly around protecting minors. Providers would also have to document their risk-mitigation measures and keep them effective, with penalties of up to A$109.2 million.

Key conclusion

This is not evidence of an immediate shift in marketing budgets from social algorithms to GEO. Its more important implication is that governments are beginning to apply formal duties, risk management, and accountability requirements to AI-mediated experiences.

That could eventually broaden GEO from an optimization discipline into a larger market around AI visibility, intelligence, accuracy, and governance.

The proposal & the feed effect

What the proposal does — and the limits of the feed argument

The starting point matters, because it prevents overinterpreting the announcement. The Australian Government describes the initiative primarily as an online-safety and user-choice measure, following its social-media age restrictions. “My Feed, My Way” is one component of a broader proposed Digital Duty of Care.

Personalized feed

Opt in to the algorithm

Platform algorithms recommend content based on the user.

Following-based feed

Opt out of recommendations

Users opt out of personally recommended content and instead see friends and creators they have chosen to follow.

The broader legislation would impose safety responsibilities on digital services, including AI chatbots, apps, games, and messaging services, particularly regarding under-18s. Providers would have to document measures addressing identified risks and ensure those measures remain effective.

Status. This remains draft legislation released for consultation, rather than a mature regulatory regime whose behavioral and economic consequences can already be measured. Consequently, any GEO impact today is primarily second-order and prospective.

The social-feed effect is weaker than it first appears

The intuitive GEO argument runs in a straight line — and one reading of the announcement leaned too heavily on it.

The intuitive chainLess social discovery → more GEO
Step 1Less algorithmic social discovery
→
Step 2Brands need alternative channels
→
Step 3Search & AI discovery matter more
→
Step 4GEO benefits

A more careful reading identifies the weakness: social recommendation feeds and generative-answer engines solve different consumer needs. Passive social discovery does not automatically convert into active AI research if recommendation feeds become less personalized. Someone who turns off personalized recommendations on a social platform does not necessarily begin asking an AI assistant what to buy.

And even if brands experienced reduced organic algorithmic reach, several alternatives could absorb their investment:

Paid social→ Creators / influencers→ Owned audiences / CRM→ Search→ GEO

GEO is therefore one potential beneficiary, not the automatic destination of displaced social investment. That substantially weakens the case for treating “My Feed, My Way” as a direct GEO growth catalyst.

A broader shift away from a single discovery algorithm

There is a more defensible structural interpretation. Brands increasingly operate across multiple discovery environments:

Traditional search

Google, Bing, and other search engines.

Social discovery

Recommendation algorithms, creators, and communities.

Direct relationships

Email, apps, subscriptions, loyalty, and CRM.

Generative discovery

AI assistants, generative search, and conversational interfaces.

The distinction that matters

The regulation may reinforce the value of diversification; it does not cause the rise of AI discovery. AI discovery and GEO are growing because consumers are adopting generative interfaces — a trend that exists independently of this Australian proposal.

The stronger signal

The stronger signal is the regulation of AI outputs

The strategically more interesting part of the announcement is that the proposed Duty of Care does not stop at social-media feeds — it explicitly extends to AI chatbots and other digital services. That matters because GEO depends on AI systems becoming important intermediaries between organizations and consumers.

Questions governments may increasingly ask

  • What information can these systems surface?
  • How do providers manage harmful or inaccurate outputs?
  • What responsibilities do providers have for vulnerable users?
  • How are risks identified and mitigated?
  • Can providers demonstrate that those controls remain effective?

Too strong to say

“Australia is regulating how brands appear in AI”

It isn’t — at least not based on this document. The proposal doesn’t establish a general regime governing brand recommendations or commercial AI answers.

The defensible conclusion

AI experiences are entering formal frameworks

AI-generated experiences are increasingly being brought inside formal digital risk and accountability frameworks. That development is relevant to the future shape of GEO.

Regulation could make GEO more valuable while making optimization harder

There is an important tension worth holding in the analysis.

Pulls demand up

Understanding AI visibility

Greater regulation of AI systems could increase enterprise demand for understanding AI visibility.

Pushes optimization down

Susceptibility to manipulation

At the same time, it could make generative systems less susceptible to deliberate optimization.

AI providers facing greater safety and compliance obligations may become more conservative about:

Unsupported claims Commercial recommendations Sensitive subjects Questionable information sources Personalized outputs Unverified content

The resulting scenario

The importance of being represented correctly in AI rises, while the ability to manipulate that representation declines. Value could migrate toward monitoring + measurement + source intelligence + content quality + governance — making GEO look less like traditional search-engine optimization and more like AI brand intelligence and digital presence management.

Authority, provenance, and information quality

The proposal requires providers to document risk-management measures and keep them effective. It does not say generative engines must favor authoritative brand content. Nevertheless, if AI providers face increasing accountability for outputs, there is a reasonable longer-term hypothesis that reliable information becomes more valuable — raising attention to:

Authoritative first-party information Consistent facts across properties Clear entities & relationships Structured product / company information Current information Credible third-party corroboration Traceable sources & provenance

This moves the discipline away from discovering ranking “tricks” and toward creating an information environment that machines can confidently understand and use — likely a more sustainable enterprise proposition.

How GEO broadens

From optimization to a broader market

“Generative Engine Optimization” naturally suggests one question — how do I increase the probability that an AI system mentions, cites, or recommends my organization? But enterprises will increasingly have another: what are AI systems saying about us, and is it accurate, current, safe, and supportable? That creates several potentially distinct markets around GEO, worth separating rather than treating everything as “GEO”:

VisibilityWhere and how often does the organization appear?
IntelligenceWhy is it appearing? Which sources seem to influence the answer?
OptimizationWhat content, entities, or information should change to improve representation?
ActivationHow are those changes created, updated, and distributed?
GovernanceIs the resulting AI representation accurate, current, substantiated, and appropriate?

Measurement may be a more durable market than optimization

One of GEO’s biggest commercial opportunities exists regardless of the Australian proposal. Traditional channels give marketers relatively mature measurement — impression → click → conversion. Generative engines disrupt that model.

The buyer’s question

Consider a consumer asking an AI: “What are the best accounting platforms for a small business?” The AI might discuss several products, explain their strengths, and ultimately influence the consumer’s shortlist.

But the brands involved may have limited visibility into:

  • whether they appeared;
  • how prominently they appeared;
  • what the AI said about them;
  • which competitors appeared;
  • what sources influenced the answer;
  • whether the information was accurate;
  • whether the interaction eventually resulted in a purchase.
Prompt monitoring→ Brand mentions→ Citations / sources→ Share of answer→ Competitive position→ Sentiment & representation→ Source attribution→ Optimization recommendations

The distinction to keep

This measurement opportunity exists with or without the Australian legislation. The regulation is a supporting signal; AI adoption is the underlying market driver.

GEO and SEO are more likely to converge than compete

Nothing in the proposal suggests conventional search becomes less important. A more likely market structure has four overlapping layers:

SEO

Optimizing visibility in traditional search.

GEO

Managing visibility and representation in generative environments.

AI visibility intelligence

Understanding how AI systems represent the organization.

AI governance

Managing accuracy, provenance, and risk.

The reframed question

The underlying problem becomes less “how do we rank on Google?” or “how do we rank in ChatGPT?” and increasingly: how do we ensure our organization is discoverable and represented correctly wherever consumers and machines seek information? That is potentially a much broader market than GEO narrowly defined.

Where the category may land

The competitive market may not remain a standalone “GEO software” category. It could evolve into a broader AI Visibility / AI Brand Intelligence / AI Presence Management market, with GEO optimization as one capability inside it.

Outlook

What would accelerate the market — and how far it spreads

Rather than using individual regulatory announcements as the primary indicator, four underlying variables are worth monitoring.

1

Consumer adoption of AI discovery

If consumers increasingly use generative interfaces for research, recommendations, and purchasing decisions, GEO becomes economically important.

2

Measurable commercial influence

Businesses need evidence that AI visibility affects consideration, traffic, leads, or revenue.

3

Observability

Companies need sufficient visibility into what generative engines say and which information sources influence them.

4

Influenceability

Organizations need credible ways to improve AI representation through better information, content, or data.

Those four factors determine GEO’s commercial potential far more directly than this particular Australian policy. Regulation becomes important primarily because it can affect the last two.

The international precedent matters more than Australia alone

The immediate market impact should be kept in perspective. Today this is:

One jurisdiction+ Draft legislation+ Consultation+ Uncertain implementation+ Uncertain consumer behavior

So the near-term effect on global GEO spending is likely very small. The strategic importance increases if the principles spread. If multiple jurisdictions begin requiring greater algorithmic choice, AI risk assessments, documentation of mitigation, accountability for AI outputs, and transparency around recommendation systems, then AI platforms may change their products globally rather than operating completely different systems in every market. At that point, regulation could materially affect the structure of the GEO market.

Framing

Australia is better viewed as a potential precedent to monitor, rather than a demand catalyst in itself.

Bottom line

Australia’s “My Feed, My Way” proposal should not be characterized as a major tailwind for the GEO market. The social-feed component is too indirect: giving consumers a choice over personalized recommendations does not mean they will shift their discovery behavior to generative engines, and any marketing spend displaced from algorithmic social reach has several alternative destinations.

The more consequential signal lies elsewhere: AI chatbots are beginning to fall within formal regulatory frameworks requiring providers to identify risks, implement controls, and demonstrate that those controls remain effective. That does not necessarily make GEO optimization easier — stronger AI governance could make generative systems harder to influence — but it could make understanding and managing AI representation substantially more important.

Two trends to separate

AI adoption is the growth engine for GEO. Regulation is a potential force shaping what GEO becomes. If generative discovery continues growing, the market is likely to expand beyond “optimizing for AI answers” toward a broader stack encompassing AI visibility, competitive intelligence, source attribution, optimization, content activation, accuracy, provenance, and governance.

That is the more defensible market implication from the Australian announcement — and it avoids attributing to this legislation a GEO growth effect that the evidence does not currently support.
